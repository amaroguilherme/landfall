## High-Level Project Summary

LANDFALL is offline-first health-monitoring software for exploration crews who cannot wait for Houston. NASA-STD-3001 sets no in-flight vital-sign alert numbers, so LANDFALL checks every crewmember against NASA's limits and NOAA's storm scale, each cited to its source, and against their own baseline at a measured chance rate, labelling every number it chose as ours. Three guided cases (radiation shelter, renal stone risk, sleep) turn a finding into one countermeasure, a tripwire, onboard escalation and a consent-filtered ground packet that waits for the link; each prints as a paper cue card. The demo puts a simulated Mars crew in a modelled solar conjunction, replays the real October 1989 proton event and re-runs the RAD team's storm rule on Curiosity's 14-year record.

## Detailed Project Description

*Figure 1. Ask Houston, or act onboard.*

### The problem: 20 light-minutes from help

MET 412/14:20:00 on the Mars surface, 18 March 2028. Mars is inside a solar-conjunction window modelled from JPL Horizons: a light-speed round trip now takes 39 min 41 s, and Houston's earliest reply is 11 d 06 h away. At 14:15 an external space-weather monitor in Mars orbit reached NOAA's S3 level (1,000 pfu, ≥10 MeV), replaying the GOES-7 record of 19 October 1989. NASA-STD-3001 has ground medical support clear crewmembers "prior to each EVA" ([V1 6006]); the earliest ground review is MET 423/20:59 (light time only), and #/eva dates each crewmember's evidence against it, clearing no one.

It has happened before. On sol 242 (11 April 2013), with Mars in solar conjunction and JPL planning to suspend commanding from 4 April to 1 May, Curiosity's RAD measured a solar particle event at Gale crater (Hassler et al. 2014), small at the surface (dose rate +24 % in iSWA's public record). #/radiation rebuilds it from NASA's archives (RAD on iSWA, STEREO-B HET on CDAWeb), beside NOAA's list.

NASA's rationale for radiation alerts: "The crew must be alerted even if ground communication is disrupted or is unviable" ([V2 6164]). Yet NASA-STD-3001 publishes no numeric in-flight alert thresholds for crew physiology (vital signs); its alert requirements leave the alert point to the program, even where Vol 2 sets the limit itself ("outside safe limits", [V2 6022], [V2 11036]; "exceed predefined thresholds", [V2 6164]). So LANDFALL labels every number with who published it; beside this claim and the BAND's searched negatives, a model-ranked list shows the closest NASA text (see Use of AI).

### How it works

**Evaluate.** NASA publishes three kinds of limit, which LANDFALL never merges: exposure ceilings (effective dose in mSv; organ doses in mGy-Eq or mGy, never converted), outcome limits and fitness-for-duty floors. Simulated CM-2's VO₂max of 35.1 ml/min/kg meets the 32.9 floor for microgravity EVA ([V1 4001]), not the 36.5 for surface EVA ([V1 4002]). *Tier A* holds 35 rules: 28 from NASA-STD-3001, verbatim, NOAA's S1–S5 scale thresholds (not limits), the HRP renal evidence report's urine target and one design choice (NOAA's S3 number at Mars). Severity follows Vol 2 Table 10.3-1; the only Warning is S3 or above. *Tier B* tests eleven alerting indicators against the crewmember's own 14-day baseline with Crawford & Howell's (1998) single-case t-test: a day outside the 99.9 % prediction interval (|t| > 4.221) is a Caution at most.

*Figure 2. One reading, two floors.*

**We audited our own statistics, twice.** The textbook Jacobson–Truax index on a crewmember's own baseline flagged an unchanged indicator on about 6.6 % of days (r = 0.8), not the 0.1 % printed; with realistic noise, two indicators raised chance cautions about 10 and 12.7 times as often as printed. Now each indicator's chance rate is measured and printed beside it: about one chance caution per crewmember every 161 days, a lower bound. In simulation, Tier B flags 1 of the 6 scripted alerting signals.

*Figure 3. Every number, its basis.*

**Act.** A trigger reaching Caution or Warning opens its case: one primary countermeasure, guided steps, a reassessment tripwire and "Why this fired". A red-flag "yes" or a tripped tripwire escalates: tier 1, the onboard Crew Medical Officer, now; tier 2, the flight surgeon, through a consent-filtered HL7 FHIR R4 packet sent "if communication is available or upon being restored" ([V2 6164] rationale). If the Crew Medical Officer is the patient, tier 1 is the Commander, the crew's second Crew Medical Officer ([V1 3006] requires at least two).

*Figure 4. Why this fired.*

Cases: a **solar particle event** (NOAA S3, or blood-forming-organ dose near its 30-day limit: stop EVA and shelter, after the vehicle's own radiation alarm, [V2 6164]); **renal stone risk** (urine below 1.5 L/day, where the HRP evidence report says risk rises: a required red-flag question first, whose "yes" escalates and holds the other steps until the Crew Medical Officer has seen the crewmember; then drink more); **sleep and circadian drift** (light per [V2 8055] Table 8.7-2).

**LANDFALL BAND.** We found no operational wearable sensor specification: the ExMC Mars medical ConOps (NTRS 20200001715) assumes "All crewmembers are wearing biosensors" and specifies none. Our answer is a block diagram and BOM, never built, referenced to the flown CSA Bio-Monitor. Its core is the NOT MEASURABLE HERE boundary, which hands urine, dose, chemistry, blood pressure and bone density to other sources. Its power switch and status light, specified for [V2 9017] and [V2 9018], show the band's state, never a health value.

**The RAD team's storm warning, replayed.** #/radiation replays the MSL RAD team's nowcasting rule (Löwe et al. 2025) on Curiosity's RAD record at NASA's iSWA (390,270 samples): of the paper's 16 Table 1 surface rows, 7 reproduce, 1 deviates, 4 of its triggers are missed and 4 non-triggers agree, and every difference is printed. On 20 May 2024 it confirms 17 minutes after onset, before any Earth reply could arrive. Of the table's 9 triggered storms at Gale within this build's Earth proton lists, only 4 had an event on the matching list within 2 days; the other 5 carried 85 % of the SEP dose the table lists for the 9 (1,917 of 2,248 µGy), most of it on 20 May 2024. This supports an onboard alarm; matching dates is not identity. The replayed October 1989 record matches NASA's design-reference storm (Table 4.8-3) in fluence within 0.5 % above 10 MeV; in hindsight, LANDFALL's S3 trigger fired when 0.56 % of that fluence had arrived.

**Paper cue cards.** [V2 10130] requires "alternative information sources for use in the event of the loss of the information management system"; its rationale names "paper cue cards". LANDFALL prints one per case (#/cards) from the engine's rules, stamped with their digest. Spacewalkers use the same idea: "a cuff checklist that they wear on their wrist", "primarily for emergencies"; if the link is lost and the suit's caution and warning system shows a message, "there is a page for it" (a NASA podcast, ep. 83). Campbell (ICES-2015-327) describes the EMU's and Advanced EMU's caution and warning systems as sharing "the use as a monitoring system not a control system". LANDFALL monitors and names one action; the crew decides.

**Coverage.** Weighted by likelihood, 13.6 % of NASA's sample Integrated Medical Model output (Vol 1 Table E.2-1, 48 conditions) is watched by a case, 23.2 % is asked daily and 6.3 % by a symptom only; 56.9 % is NOT MEASURABLE HERE. Watched or asked does not mean detected.

### What we hope to achieve

The Crew Medical Officer ("CMOs often are, but are not required, to be physicians", [V1 5003] rationale) gets one plain-English action and its reason; flight surgeons, a coded packet; programs, the alert points they must set. Human Research Roadmap gap EIHSO-301 reads: "We need to determine the on-board, intelligent systems that will support crew health and performance, and we need to establish the thresholds that will define how these systems should be implemented (including in-mission and at landing)." LANDFALL proposes one such system; its thresholds are data a program can replace. Next: analog-crew data, a bench-built BAND, and usability tests with non-physicians on NASA's NMSUS, Bedford and Table 5.1-1 measures (docs/USABILITY_TEST.md).

The idea comes from navigation: NASA tested a sextant on the ISS because "crews need the capability to navigate autonomously in the event of lost communication with the ground" (PI Greg Holt). LANDFALL is that backup for health.

## Use of NASA Data

Remove the NASA data and LANDFALL loses its Tier A rules, its light time and conjunction, its radiation record, its hazard and risk text and its real lab ranges. Build-time adapters freeze each source as JSON with retrieval time, SHA-256 and caveats; all 27 snapshots are listed at #/data. NASA does not endorse this project, and no NASA insignia is used.

- **NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C** (NASA OCHMO, approved 2023-09-15). The verbatim text of 20 requirements, the FFD/PEL/POL glossary, Tables 4.8-1 and 4.8-3 and Appendix E's Table E.2-1, extracted with a ToUnicode-aware PDF parser from a PDF identified by SHA-256. It drives the FFD ([V1 4001], [V1 4002]), POL ([V1 4027]) and PEL rules ([V1 4030], the 600 mSv career effective-dose limit; Table 4.8-1 organ limits in mGy-Eq or mGy, never converted to mSv), the vehicle-design context rules ([V1 4031]–[V1 4033], shown but never fired), the in-mission fitness-for-duty and sensorimotor limits it leaves to operations ([V1 4006], [V1 4008]), the care duties the cases and the BAND cite ([V1 3003], [V1 3004]), the crew's second Crew Medical Officer ([V1 3006]: "a minimum of two crewmembers per vehicle/platform", "to ensure that a backup is available for medical treatment") and ground medical clearance before each EVA ([V1 6006], cited on both VO₂max rules, quoted in "The problem" and shown verbatim on #/eva). The [V1 5003] rationale quoted in "What we hope to achieve" was read on p. 46 of the same PDF; it is not in the frozen snapshot. Table E.2-1, NASA's sample Integrated Medical Model output (48 conditions with a likelihood per mission), weights the coverage score, read with NASA's own limitation (E.2.3): "One limitation is that IMM was developed for application to ISS missions, but it may be applied to other design reference missions if the appropriate limitations are considered during interpretation of results." Table 4.8-3, the design-reference SPE environment (PDF p. 42; 100 energy/fluence pairs, "Sum of the October 1989 Events"), checks the replay: the GOES-7 record agrees with it in fluence within 0.5 % above 10 MeV and 2.6 % above 100 MeV (a consistency check; NASA does not say how the table was derived), and ending the event window anywhere from 26 to 31 October 1989 moves that to between −1.9 % and +0.5 % above 10 MeV and between 0.0 % and +2.7 % above 100 MeV. In hindsight, LANDFALL's S3 trigger fired when 0.56 % of the ≥10 MeV fluence (3.6 % above 100 MeV) had arrived; by the RAD team's lead-time measure, 10 % had arrived 18.4 h later above 10 MeV and 4.0 h later above 100 MeV. *In the app:* the category and citation on every finding, #/body, #/provenance, #/method (coverage: #/method?s=coverage), #/radiation (the design-reference check).

## Use of Artificial Intelligence

Team Isaac Clarke is Guilherme Amaro (project lead) and Ayra Castilho (tester). The team is named after Isaac Clarke, the starship systems engineer of the Dead Space video games, whose suit shows his overall health as one bar on its back so that co-workers can monitor it; LANDFALL makes different choices: no single health score, a private mood check-in, a ground packet filtered by each crewmember's consent, and a BAND whose one light shows the band's state, never its wearer's health. Guilherme built LANDFALL with Claude Code (Anthropic) as the coding assistant: he set the scope and the design, made the decisions and reviewed the results. Under his direction, Claude Code:

- wrote most of the code: the Python engine and its tests, the ingest adapters, the TypeScript port, the front end, the cue-card printer and the BAND generator;
- ran the build-time data ingestion;
- ran verification agents that re-read primary sources (standards PDFs, NTRS reports, OSDR files, NOAA, JPL and iSWA data, the Löwe et al. preprint) against our claims, and audited the statistics;
- ran a review agent that read the 48 model-ranked NASA statements described below against their PDF pages; all matched;
- wrote the test questions that measure the model's two rankings (31 and 24 questions: agent-written, not crew-written);
- drafted the documentation, including drafts of this page.

During preparation, AI research agents also searched and summarised background sources.

We treated AI output as claims to check. The checks caught real errors:

- a wrong approval date for NASA-STD-3001 Vol 2 Rev F in our own plan;
- a urine-output target in the plan with no NASA source, replaced by the HRP evidence report's numbers;
- a quote credited to NASA's Standard Measures page that the page does not contain;
- an AI web-page summary that invented an OCHMO "Wearables & Sensors" section (since then, every snapshot the app ships is downloaded and parsed by code);
- the Tier B false-alarm understatements described above, which earlier builds printed;
- a brief that called two RAD triggers "under 1 µGy" (they total 1.4 and 1.2 µGy);
- a draft that cut NASA's IMM limitation sentence short, before "but it may be applied to other design reference missions";
- our Vol 2 claim of "no data storage or log-duration requirement": the model-ranked list surfaced [V2 6020], which requires cabin atmosphere data to be recorded automatically, so the claim now reads "no storage-capacity, retention or log-duration requirement".

No AI is in the decision path: every rule is data you can read at #/provenance, evaluated by deterministic code that gives the same outputs in Python and in the browser. One small open model is used outside it, in two places: potion-base-8M (MinishLab, MIT; static word vectors distilled from BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5; revision bf8b0566). It generates no text, decides nothing and reads no crew data, and nothing else in LANDFALL reads its output.

- **At build time**, for each searched negative and for the missing vital-sign alert threshold, it ranked 543 of the 545 requirements of NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C and Vol 2 Rev F (our PDF parsers miss [V1 5002] and [V2 10157]) by closeness in meaning to one sentence we wrote before running it, to show where those claims come closest to being wrong. #/band and #/method?s=gap list the five closest, verbatim and by rank only, under the deterministic keyword count, which stays the claim. It is a ranking, not proof of absence. Where we had already named the nearest requirement by hand, it ranked 1 of those 6 in its top five; on the 31 test questions, the expected requirement was among its five for 23.
- **In the browser**, on #/method?s=find ("Find it in NASA's words"), it ranks the 57 frozen NASA-STD-3001 requirements by closeness to your words, beside a keyword search that is shown first, and returns only NASA's text. Its 8-bit copy (7.9 MB) is not precached and loads on the first search. A fixed word list runs on the same words, model or not: renal red flags and urgent symptoms get "tell your Crew Medical Officer now (if that is you, the Commander)", and medication, diagnosis and cancer-risk words get the refusals written in the rules. Its hand-written TypeScript tokenizer was tested equal to the original on every fixture string and, for normalising and splitting, on all 1,112,064 code points under Node 25.4 (tools/find/README.md). On the 24 test questions, written blind to the search tool before its first run, the expected requirement was in its top three for 20 (keywords alone: 16; either column: 22). A ranking is not an answer.

No image, audio or video generator was used: the body figure and the BAND diagram are SVG drawn by code, the figures are screen captures of the app, and the demo video is captured from the app by a script, with HTML title cards and no audio [VIDEO-TBC]. AI-assisted checking does not replace review by subject-matter experts, which is why every number links to its source.
